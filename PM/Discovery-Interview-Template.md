# Discovery Interview Template — Reusable

> **How to use this template**
> Two tiers in one place:
> - **PART A — Core Interview**: The minimum framework for every user interview. Always use this.
> - **PART B — Extended Sections**: Add for JTBD research, synthesis, continuous discovery programs.
>
> **Rules of thumb**
> - Talk about their *past behavior*, not their *future intentions*. "Tell me about the last time…" not "Would you use…"
> - One interview per week minimum (Teresa Torres' continuous discovery). Research isn't a one-off sprint.
> - Saturation signals: same themes appearing 80%+ of the time, no new "aha" moments. Typical: 8–12 interviews per segment.
> - The Mom Test: Ask questions their mother would answer honestly even if they love you. No leading, no pitching, no validating.
> - Delete this instruction block and italic *guiding prompts* once filled.

## 4. Interview Flow

### Opening (2–3 min)

*Script (adapt to your tone):*
> "Thanks for your time. I'm [name] — I work on [product / area].  
> I'm trying to understand how people like you handle [problem area]. This isn't a product pitch — I just want to learn from your experience.  
> I may take notes; is it OK if I record this for my own reference only?  
> Do you have about 45 minutes?"

### Context Building (8–10 min)

*Goal: Understand who they are, their role, and their environment.*

- "Tell me about your current role and what a typical day looks like."
- "What does success look like for you in your position?"
- "Who do you typically work with? Who influences your decisions?"

*Note: Role, seniority, team size, tooling environment.*

---

### Problem Exploration (20–25 min)

*Goal: Understand the problem through their actual past behavior. Never hypotheticals.*

**Opening:**
- "Tell me about the last time you encountered [core problem area]."
- "Walk me through what happened — what were you trying to accomplish?"

**Probing their behavior:**
- "What did you try first? What happened next?"
- "How long did that take?"
- "What have you tried before? How long did you stick with it?"
- "Why didn't that work?"

**Understanding frequency and severity:**
- "How often does this happen?"
- "How much time or money does this cost you?"
- "What's the worst it's ever gotten?"

*Do NOT ask: "Would you use a product that…?" or "Don't you think [X] is a problem?"*

---

### Current Solution Exploration (10–12 min)

*Goal: Understand their workaround or current tool in detail.*

- "Walk me through how you currently handle [task]."
- "What works well about your current approach?"
- "What's frustrating or missing?"
- "Why haven't you switched to something different?"
- "What have you already tried? What made you stop using it?"

---

### Decision Factors (5–8 min)

*Goal: Understand what would make them switch.*

- "When you're evaluating new tools or approaches, what matters most to you?"
- "Who else would need to be convinced?"
- "What would it need to cost?"
- "What would have to be true for you to try something new?"

---

### Closing (2–3 min)

> "This has been really helpful. Is there anything I missed or that you think I should understand?"  
> "Who else should I talk to about this?"  
> "Can I reach out if I have follow-up questions?"

*Do NOT pitch your product unless they ask directly. "What are you building?" → "I'm exploring this space — still early. Happy to share more once we've talked to enough people."*

# PART B — EXTENDED SECTIONS
*(Add for deeper research programs or structured synthesis.)*

## B1. Question Type Reference

*Use this to build custom question lists for specific discovery goals.*

| Type | Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Open-ended** | Invite storytelling, reveal unexpected paths | "Tell me about…", "Walk me through…", "Describe a time when…" |
| **Probing** | Go deeper into what they said | "What happened next?", "Why did you choose that?", "Can you give me an example?" |
| **Clarifying** | Confirm your understanding | "Just to check — are you saying that X happens weekly?", "When you say 'expensive,' what cost are we talking?" |
| **Silence** | Let them think and continue | *(Ask question, wait 8–10 seconds before rephrasing)* |
| **Comparative** | Understand trade-offs | "How does your current approach compare to [alternative]?" |
| **Chronological** | Reconstruct past events | "What did you do first? And then? How long did that take?" |

**NEVER use:**
- Leading questions: "Don't you think X is a problem?" → reveals your hypothesis
- Hypothetical behavior: "Would you use a tool that…?" → people answer to be polite
- Multiple questions at once: "What do you think about X, and also how often does Y happen?" → pick one

## B2. The Mom Test — Quick Reference

*From Rob Fitzpatrick's "The Mom Test": Questions your mom would answer honestly even if she loves you.*

| Ask This | Not This |
|---|---|
| "How do you currently handle X?" | "Would you use a product that did X?" |
| "What have you already tried?" | "Do you think this is a good idea?" |
| "How much does this cost you in time/money?" | "Would you pay for something that fixed this?" |
| "Tell me about the last time this happened." | "Imagine you could do X — would you?" |
| "What's the #1 thing you wish you could change?" | "What features would you want?" |

## B3. Jobs-to-Be-Done (JTBD) Framework

*Shifts focus from "who the user is" to "what job they're trying to accomplish."*

**JTBD Interview Structure:**

1. **Open context** — "Tell me about the last time you needed to [accomplish job]."
2. **Situation** — "What were you trying to accomplish? What triggered it?"
3. **Motivation** — "Why was this important at that moment?"
4. **Search & evaluation** — "How did you figure out what to do? What did you try?"
5. **Decision** — "Why did you choose that approach over others?"
6. **Outcome** — "How did it work out? Are you still using it?"

**Three job layers to uncover:**

| Job Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | The core task to complete | "Schedule team meetings" |
| **Emotional** | How they want to feel | "Feel organized and in control" |
| **Social** | How they want to be perceived | "Be seen as efficient and reliable" |

## B4. Reading Signals vs. Opinions

*What people say ≠ what they do.*

| Opinion (Lower Reliability) | Behavioral Signal to Look For (Higher Reliability) |
|---|---|
| "I'd definitely use this" | Have they spent money or time on something similar? |
| "This is a huge problem" | How much time/money do they spend on it now? |
| "I'd pay $50/month for this" | What are they currently paying for similar solutions? |
| "We need this ASAP" | How long have they tolerated the current solution? |
| "I keep meaning to switch" | What's actually stopped them from switching? |

**Reveal actual behavior:**
- "How much time do you spend on this weekly?"
- "What have you already tried? How long did you stick with it?"
- "Have you ever searched for a solution? What did you find?"

## B6. Synthesis Method: Affinity Mapping

*For teams synthesizing 5+ interviews.*

**Process:**
1. Extract 1–3 insights from each interview onto separate notes (one insight per note)
2. Cluster similar themes (don't force clusters — let them emerge)
3. Name each cluster with a behavioral insight, not a label
4. Count frequency (how many interviews mention this cluster?)
5. Flag outliers (single-mention unexpected findings)

**Cluster categories to consider:**
- Jobs (what are they trying to accomplish?)
- Pain points (what makes it hard?)
- Current solutions (how are they solving it now?)
- Constraints (what limits better solutions?)
- Success metrics (how do they measure being done?)
- Decision factors (what influences their choices?)

## B7. Interview Saturation Guide

*When can you stop interviewing?*

| Stage | Interview Count | Expected New Insights |
|---|---|---|
| Early discovery | 1–5 | 50–60% new per interview |
| Pattern forming | 6–8 | 20–30% new per interview |
| Approaching saturation | 9–12 | 5–10% new per interview |
| Saturated | 13+ | Mostly confirming existing themes |

**Saturation signals:**
- Same themes appear in 80%+ of interviews
- You can predict what the next person will say
- New interviews no longer generate "aha" moments
- You're hearing the same stories and examples

**Per goal:**
- Single hypothesis validation: 5–8 interviews
- Broad problem exploration: 10–15 interviews
- Per segment (different user type): 8–12 per segment

*Saturation ≠ "I understand the whole market." It means "I've found the common patterns in this segment."*

## B8. Common PM Interview Mistakes

| Mistake | Why It Hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pitching instead of listening | Kills honest feedback; biases toward validation | Talk about their life, not your idea |
| Asking about future behavior | People answer to be polite; not predictive | Ask about past behavior only |
| Recruiting only fans / current users | Sampling bias; misses objections | Include churned users, non-users, skeptics |
| Filling silence | Prevents deep thinking; you answer for them | Ask, then wait 10+ seconds |
| Ignoring contradictions | Misses real priorities | Bring it back: "Earlier you said X, but now Y — help me understand" |
| Interviewing only your network | Proximity bias; they already like you | Cold recruit, panel access, go where users are |
| Solo synthesis | One PM's interpretation misses patterns others see | Team synthesis; shared insight board |
| One-off research sprint | Problem space evolves; you go stale | Continuous discovery: 1 interview/week minimum |
